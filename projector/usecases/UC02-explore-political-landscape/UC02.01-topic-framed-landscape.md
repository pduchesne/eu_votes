# UC02.01 — Navigate the Landscape with Subjects as Axes

A user who cannot say what "axis 2" means can say what they think about migration,
budgets or foreign policy. This use case gives them the same landscape framed by subjects
instead of principal components: they pick three themes, and the point cloud places every
member by where they sit on each of those three subjects. Selecting one subject shows how
each political group spreads along its axis, and the texts that anchor either end.

The principal-component view (`UC02`) remains the more faithful description — it explains
more of the variation and is the one the methodology defends. This is the legible one, and
the trade is stated on the page rather than hidden: a topic axis explains less, and its
direction is no less arbitrary.

## Why an axis, not a for-against score
The obvious version of this — "how often did they vote in favour of texts on migration" —
is wrong, and measurably so. Restrictive and permissive proposals on a subject both come
to a vote, so a yes-count puts the supporters of opposing texts at the same end. A
component instead gives every vote a signed weight learned from who votes together, so
backing a restrictive text and backing a permissive one pull in opposite directions.

Measured on the 9th term's asylum votes, the two texts defining either end carry near
mirror-image weights (−0.082 and +0.083), and S&D voted for the first 100% of the time and
the second 0%, with ID doing the exact reverse. Correlation between the naive yes-count
and the topic axis reaches +0.90 on social policy but **−0.43** on foreign and security
policy, where it inverts outright. The published data therefore carries that correlation
per subject, and the interface says when the simple reading would mislead.

## What the user can do
- Choose any three subjects as the three axes of the 3D cloud, and orbit it.
- Read, per subject: votes and members behind it, how much of the disagreement on that
  subject its axis accounts for, and how closely it tracks the overall landscape.
- Select a subject and see each group's distribution along it as a ridgeline.
- Read the texts anchoring each end, each with how the two extreme groups voted on it.
- Click a member to open their record (`UC01`), or follow the subject to its theme page.

## Choosing the frame is a correctness question, not a cosmetic one
First attempt defaulted to the three busiest subjects. That was wrong, and measurably:
nearly every subject's axis correlates 0.93–0.98 with the chamber's first component, so
the busiest three all describe the same division and the 3D view drew it three times
over. The second and third components of the main landscape never appeared at all.

Each topic axis is therefore placed as a direction in the main three-component space, and
the default three are chosen to span it — the triple whose directions come closest to
perpendicular, drawn from subjects with at least 150 verified votes so the axes are
themselves well determined. Measured as the determinant of the three unit directions,
where 1.00 is three mutually perpendicular divisions:

| Term | Busiest three | Spanning frame |
|---|---|---|
| 8 | 0.31 | **0.47** |
| 9 | **0.01** | **0.45** |
| 10 | 0.07 | **0.19** |

The reader may still pick any three, and the page recomputes and states what their choice
spans — including telling them plainly when it has collapsed.

Rejected alternative: judging a frame by how much member position three topic scores can
*reconstruct*. Least squares recovers a lot from a badly conditioned basis by amplifying
small differences between near-parallel axes — the busiest three in the 10th term score
89% on that measure while spanning 0.07 — but no amount of amplification makes a
collapsed picture readable. Span is the property a visual frame needs.

## Finding that came out of building it
The same cleavage recurs inside almost every subject. In the 9th term 33 of 38 subjects
lie closest to the first main axis, five to the second, and **none** to the third — so
the third axis is not any policy area's internal disagreement but something cutting
across them, and no choice of subjects can frame it. That is why even the best topic
frame covers under half the space, and the interface says so rather than implying the
named axes are a full substitute for the computed ones.
